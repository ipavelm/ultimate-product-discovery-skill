# Customer Development for a new market

This file is the alternative route through blocks II and IV for projects whose market is classified as "New" (task 1 in `block-1-market.md`). Read it when the market type is "New". For existing and resegmented markets, carry on with the standard modules and skip this file.

## Why an alternative route is needed

Standard PD assumes customers are already looking for a solution to their problem, and the only question is which solution they "hire". On a new market that is not the case: the audience **does not yet recognise the problem**. The classic example: before the iPhone, nobody was "looking for" a multitouch smartphone — people typed SMS on keypad phones and considered that normal.

In that situation:
- **The Smoke Test converts badly** — not because the offer is weak, but because nobody is searching for a solution to this problem in advertising channels
- **A pre-sale is impossible** — customers will not pay for something they do not yet see as necessary
- **Interviews only work if framed correctly** — you cannot ask "would you buy X"; you have to find out how they cope **today** without X

Steve Blank (Four Steps to the Epiphany) described a protocol that does work here. The essence: split PD into 4 phases, with most of the effort going into **confirming the pain exists** rather than into selling the product.

## The 4 phases of Customer Development

### 1. Customer Discovery (2–4 weeks)

**Goal:** confirm the pain exists — not that the product is wanted.

Instead of a Smoke Test, run 15–25 in-depth interviews following The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick). The method's key rule: **no hypotheses about the product in the first half of the interview**; only questions about past situations, current coping strategies, the pain, and what it costs (time, money, reputation).

Questions that are off limits at this stage:
- "Would you buy a product like this?" — the answer tells you nothing; people agree out of politeness
- "What matters to you in [category]?" — abstract answers, unconnected to real behaviour
- "If a tool X existed, would you use it?" — the same abstraction problem

The right questions:
- "Tell me about the last time you ran into [situation]"
- "How did you handle it?"
- "What was the hardest part?"
- "What did you do to avoid it happening again?"
- "How much time or money did it cost you?"

Output of the phase: confirmation that the pain is real, affects a meaningful number of people, and that they are willing to spend resources reducing it. If it is not there, go back to Step 0 and restate the problem hypothesis.

### 2. Customer Validation (4–8 weeks)

**Goal:** find people willing to pay for the outcome (not for the product), and deliver that outcome by hand.

Instead of a full MVP, run Concierge: do the customer's job yourself, for money, with no automation. You are looking for 3–5 earlyvangelist customers — people who at the same time:
- Recognise the pain (you do not have to convince them)
- Are actively searching, or have already cobbled together their own workaround (a spreadsheet hack, three services wired together, a hired assistant)
- Have a budget for a solution
- Are willing to pay for the outcome even when it arrives as an email from a human

If you cannot find 3–5 of them in 8 weeks, that is a signal the market is not ready or the problem hypothesis is too broad. Pivot into a narrower segment.

### 3. Customer Creation

**Goal:** start paying to teach the market that the problem exists.

Only **after** Customer Validation do you launch content, advertising, PR and educational material. The job is not to sell — it is to establish the category "problems of type X" in the audience's heads and show that they can be solved. Standard product marketing does not work here; educational content, first-customer stories and public discussion do.

At this stage CAC runs 3–5x above the benchmarks for mature markets. That is normal — build it into the financial model.

### 4. Company Building

**Goal:** move to scaling once Customer Creation has produced visible signals (organic growth, inbound enquiries, mentions you did not prompt).

From here the classic scaling practices apply — formalising sales, hiring, process.

## Practical consequences for the financial model

When the market type is "New", the financial plan's defaults do not hold. Adjust as follows:

| Parameter | Standard | New market |
|-----------|----------|------------|
| Planning horizon | 12 months is enough | 24 months minimum (6 months goes to Customer Discovery) |
| Revenue in months 1–6 | From the funnel | Close to zero — that is normal, build it into runway |
| CAC | Industry benchmark | Benchmark × 3–5 (you are paying to educate the market) |
| LTV/CAC ≥ 3x | By month 6 | By month 12, not month 6 |
| Smoke Test conversion | 3–8% B2C | 0.5–2% — not a signal of a bad offer but of immature demand |

## When to return to the standard route

Once Customer Validation (phase 2) is complete, most tasks in blocks III and IV become applicable in their standard form — you now have data from 3–5 real paying customers, and it can feed the JTBD, the OST and the hypothesis pool.

Customer Creation (phase 3) uses the same Opportunity Solution Tree as task 12; the business outcome is simply framed differently ("raise awareness of the problem in segment X from 5% to 20% over 12 months" instead of "raise retention from 20% to 35%").
